How to Calculate Percentage Increase and Decrease

The plain-English breakdown of percentage change formulas, step-by-step math for raises and price drops, and why starting values trip everyone up.

7 min read Published September 2026 By NexAlc
Last reviewed: September 2026
Quick Answer: Percentage change measures how much a value shifts relative to where it started. The universal equation for percentage change is: ((New Value - Original Value) / Original Value) × 100. A positive result indicates a percentage increase, while a negative result signals a percentage decrease.

Raw numbers rarely tell the whole story. A $10 price jump means little until knowing if the item is a movie ticket or a laptop. Percentages bridge that gap instantly by putting every shift in context.

Whether evaluating an upcoming raise, tracking grocery inflation, or checking a clearance discount, calculating percentage shifts is one of the most practical everyday math skills anyone can master. Yet, countless people freeze when looking at the formula or second-guess whether to divide by the beginning number or the ending number.

The underlying arithmetic is straightforward once stripped of jargon. Each step below demonstrates how ratios work, why your baseline anchors everything, and how to catch retail tricks.

What Is Percentage Change? (And Why People Always Google It)

Percentage change expresses the relative difference between an older baseline figure and a newer final figure as a proportion of one hundred parts. Unlike simple subtraction, which reveals only the raw numeric gap, percentage change tells you how meaningful that gap actually is relative to the starting point.

Consider two everyday situations: suppose a commuter transit pass jumps by $2, rising from $2 to $4. Next, imagine a laptop jumps by that exact same $2, rising from $1,000 to $1,002. In both cases, the absolute change is identical: exactly two dollars. But for your wallet, those two changes feel worlds apart. The transit pass doubled in cost, representing a 100% price hike. The laptop barely moved, recording a 0.2% increase. Percentage change captures that vital distinction by anchoring the difference against the original number.

Searches for percentage change peak each month because daily decisions demand clarity. People evaluate whether annual raises outpace inflation, track company revenue, or check price tags before checkout. A reliable framework prevents expensive mistakes.

The Percentage Increase Formula

Calculating a percentage increase answers a core question: relative to the starting figure, what fraction was added? The standard formula divides that difference by the original baseline.

Percentage Increase = ((New Value - Original Value) / Original Value) × 100

The numerator isolates numeric gain. Dividing by the original baseline gives a decimal ratio, which multiplying by 100 turns into a percentage. Alternatively, divide new by old, subtract 1, and multiply by 100:

Percentage Increase = ((New Value / Original Value) - 1) × 100

Both methods give identical results. For instance, what is the percentage increase if 250 is increased to 300? Subtracting 250 from 300 gives 50. Dividing 50 by baseline 250 yields 0.20, or 20%. Similarly, how to calculate 20% increase on any baseline? Multiply by 1.20, turning $50 into $60. What is a 5% increase of $100? Multiply 100 × 1.05 to reach $105.

The Percentage Decrease Formula

A percentage decrease measures how much a value dropped relative to its start. The math mirrors the increase formula, with subtraction reversed to keep the reduction positive.

Percentage Decrease = ((Original Value - New Value) / Original Value) × 100

The equation ((New - Original) / Original) × 100 yields a negative value when numbers drop, signaling reduction. In everyday speech, people drop the minus sign and state the discount as a positive percentage.

Shoppers frequently ask how to calculate a 20% reduction during clearance sales. A 20% discount means paying 80% of original cost. Multiply the starting price by 0.80: boots tagged at $110 cost 110 × 0.80 = $88, saving $22. Similarly, knowing how to do a 40% decrease uses multiplier 0.60: a $180 room reduced 40% is 180 × 0.60 = $108. What is 43 decreased by 26%? Multiply 43 × 0.74 = 31.82.

Step-by-Step: How to Calculate Percentage Increase and Decrease

Following a reliable four-step sequence eliminates confusion, especially when numbers feature odd decimals.

Calculating Percentage Increase

Step 1: Identify your numbers. Label starting figure as original value and higher resulting figure as new value. Starting figure must always be the denominator.

Step 2: Subtract original from new value. Compute New Value - Original Value to find numeric gain.

Step 3: Divide by original value. Divide gain by starting baseline to produce decimal ratio.

Step 4: Multiply by 100. Shift decimal two places right to express answer as percentage.

Calculating Percentage Decrease

Step 1: Confirm baseline and lower final figure. Anchor your math to where the number stood before the drop.

Step 2: Subtract new value from original value. Compute Original Value - New Value to isolate numeric drop.

Step 3: Divide by original value. Divide reduction by starting baseline, never by ending figure.

Step 4: Multiply by 100. Multiply decimal by 100 to state percentage reduction clearly.

Real-World Example: Salary, Prices, and Everyday Math

Consider an employee earning $48,000 whose compensation increases to $54,000. Evaluating if this matches market inflation requires calculating the percentage raise.

The baseline salary is $48,000, and new salary is $54,000. Subtracting $48,000 from $54,000 gives a $6,000 annual raise. Next, divide that $6,000 gain by original salary $48,000:

6,000 / 48,000 = 0.125

Multiplying 0.125 by 100 delivers an exact 12.5% salary increase. If inflation hovered at 3.5% over that period, pay outpaced rising costs by 9.0 percentage points, translating to real purchasing power growth.

Compare that to retail grocery bills. If coffee jumps from $12.50 to $15.00, the $2.50 hike divided by $12.50 is a 20% jump. A $2.50 change sounds small, but 20% across weekly shopping compounds quickly.

Positive vs. Negative Percentage Change — What It Actually Means

A positive percentage indicates growth above baseline. A negative percentage denotes contraction, discounts, or losses. Beyond basic arithmetic, positive and negative changes carry mathematical asymmetry.

Percentages do not scale symmetrically in opposite directions because the baseline moves after every shift. If a $100 portfolio drops 50%, it falls to $50. Gaining 50% next quarter adds $25, reaching only $75. Breaking even requires a 100% gain ($50 rising back to $100).

Percentage Increase
Growth Dynamics
  • Unbounded ceiling (can exceed 100%, 500%, or 10,000%)
  • Denominator is smaller than final numerator amount
  • Compounding growth builds on expanding baselines
  • Describes wage gains, asset returns, and inflation rates
Percentage Decrease
Reduction Dynamics
  • Naturally bounded at 100% for standard real assets
  • Denominator is larger than final numerator amount
  • Requires larger percentage gains to recover ground
  • Describes retail markdowns, depreciation, and pullbacks

This asymmetry matters when shopping sales. A store claiming prices "50% below competitor" implies the rival charges twice as much (a 100% markup). Downward percentage cuts always require larger upward gains to reverse.

Common Mistakes People Make (And How to Avoid Them)

Most errors in percentage calculations stem from confusing reference baselines rather than flawed math.

Dividing by new number instead of original number. This is the most frequent mistake. When a stock climbs from $20 to $25, dividing by 25 gives 20%. But stock started at $20, so true calculation is 5 / 20 = 25%. Dividing by ending figure distorts historical change.

Confusing percentage points with percentage change. If mortgage rates rise from 6.0% to 7.0%, they increased by 1.0 percentage point. However, percentage increase of borrowing cost is (7 - 6) / 6 = 16.67%. Conflating these leads people to severely underestimate interest spikes.

Assuming sequential percentage changes cancel each other out. Taking 20% off a price and adding 20% back on does not return to original amount. A $100 jacket discounted 20% costs $80. Adding 20% back to $80 brings it to $96, leaving you $4 short of starting price.

Overlooking zero or negative baselines. Formulas break down when starting value is zero because division by zero is undefined. If revenue moves from $0 to $10,000, you report absolute dollar growth rather than a percentage.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate a percentage increase? ▾
Subtract original value from new value, divide by original value, and multiply by 100. For example, moving from $50 to $65 is ((65 - 50) / 50) × 100 = 30%.
How do I calculate a percentage decrease? ▾
Subtract new value from original baseline, divide by original baseline, and multiply by 100. Dropping from $80 to $60 gives ((80 - 60) / 80) × 100 = 25%.
How to calculate percentage change? ▾
Use the equation ((New Value - Old Value) / Old Value) × 100. A positive result indicates growth, while negative indicates decline.
How to calculate 20% increase? ▾
Multiply the original number by 1.20. For example, a 20% increase on $150 gives 150 × 1.20 = $180.
What is a 5% increase of $100? ▾
A 5% increase of $100 is $105. Either add 5% ($5) to $100, or multiply directly: 100 × 1.05 = $105.
What is the percentage increase if 250 is increased to 300? ▾
The percentage increase from 250 to 300 is 20%. The calculation is ((300 - 250) / 250) × 100 = (50 / 250) × 100 = 20%.
What is 43 decreased by 26%? ▾
43 decreased by 26% is 31.82. Multiply 43 by 0.74 (which is 1 - 0.26) to get 31.82 directly.
How to do a 40% decrease? ▾
Multiply original amount by 0.60 (since 100% - 40% = 60%). A $200 item discounted 40% costs 200 × 0.60 = $120.
